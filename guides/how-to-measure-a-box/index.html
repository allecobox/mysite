How to Measure a Box (The Way a Factory Does It)

Nine out of ten quote errors start with the wrong measurement. Here is how to measure a box in internal dimensions, how much the board adds on the outside, and how much clearance your product actually needs — with the numbers we use on the factory floor.

By AllecoBox · Updated 21 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

The one rule: always quote internal dimensions as Length × Width × Height, where length is the longer side of the opening, width the shorter side, and height the vertical depth. A 200 × 150 × 80 mm box fits a product up to 200 mm long, 150 mm wide and 80 mm tall — inside, not outside.

Step 1 — Decide inside or outside (and stop mixing them)

Every factory prices and prints from internal dimensions, because the inside is what your product has to fit into. The outside is a derived number: it is the inside plus twice the board thickness on each axis. Mix the two and you end up with a box that is 3–6 mm too tight — enough to stop a lid closing or crack a jar during transit.

Step 2 — Measure the product, not the old box

If you already have packaging you like, measure it — but check the product too. Old boxes are often oversized because the previous supplier used a stock size. Take the three largest points of the product:

  • Length — the longest dimension, including any cap, pump or handle that sticks out
  • Width — the second dimension at its widest point, not the body
  • Height — from the base to the highest point; for bottles this includes the cap, for boxes it includes the lid

Measure at the widest point of each axis, and measure again after you have fitted the product the way it will ship. A serum bottle lying down has a different footprint to the same bottle standing up.

Step 3 — Add the clearance your product needs

Clearance is the air gap between product and wall. Too little and the box won't close or the product gets scratched; too much and it rattles around in transit. These are the allowances we use, per axis:

Product typeClearance per axisNotes
Rigid item, no insert (candle, book, boxed toy)3–5 mmEnough to slide in and out without forcing
Glass bottle / jar standing upright4–6 mm + insertAdd a neck or base insert; glass needs vertical support, not just side clearance
Cosmetic tube, lipstick, compact2–4 mmTight fit reads as premium — but keep 2 mm minimum or the print scuffs
Apparel, folded textiles8–12 mmSoft goods compress; too tight and the folds crease permanently
Electronics with foam/EVA insert6–10 mmThe insert itself takes 10–15 mm; size the box around the insert, not the device
Food / bakery (cake, pastry, chocolate)5–8 mmPlus 10–15 mm height if a dome or paper collar is used
Subscription mailer (multiple items)10–15 mmLeave headroom so the fold line does not split when packed full

Rule of thumb: box internal = product + 2 × clearance. A 100 mm wide bottle with 5 mm clearance each side needs a box at least 110 mm wide.

Corrugated and rigid board are not paper-thin. If you need the outside dimensions — for shipping cartons, pallet planning or shelf-fit checks — add twice the wall thickness to each axis:

Five measuring mistakes that ruin a print run

  1. Quoting outside dimensions. The single most common error. A 200 mm outside box is roughly a 196 mm inside box — sometimes not enough.
  2. Measuring the flat dieline. A dieline includes flaps and glue tabs. Those tabs are not part of the finished box's internal size.
  3. Ignoring lid depth on two-piece boxes. The lid slides over the base, so the base must be smaller than the lid's internal size by one board thickness on each side. Get this wrong and the lid either won't go on or falls off.
  4. Forgetting the insert. A 15 mm foam insert eats 15 mm of internal length and width. Size the box around the insert.
  5. Rounding to nice numbers. Rounding 187 mm up to 200 mm is fine; rounding down is not. Always round up, never down.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I measure a box inside or outside?

Always inside. A factory quotes and prints from internal dimensions, because the inside is what the product has to fit into. Outside dimensions are the internal size plus twice the board thickness on each axis, and are only used for shipping or shelf-fit calculations.

How much bigger should the box be than the product?

Add 3–5 mm per axis for rigid products without an insert, 4–6 mm plus an insert for glass bottles, 8–12 mm for folded apparel, and 10–15 mm for subscription mailers packed with several items. Box internal size = product size + twice the clearance.

What if my product is soft or flexible?

Measure it lightly compressed, the way it will sit in the box, not pulled tight. Soft goods need 8–12 mm per axis so the folds do not take a permanent crease, and so the box still closes when the item is packed slightly off-centre.

Can you measure the box from a photo of my current packaging?

Yes, if the photo includes a ruler or a common object for scale, and you tell us roughly how thick the board is. Photos are good enough for an indicative quote; we confirm the real measurement on a physical sample before bulk production.
